How this experience was made

This page exists so that anyone can know which part of the project is human research and judgement, which part is machine-assisted production, and exactly where the line between them runs.

Prior research

The journey starts from a complete reading of the epic and a selection of twelve episodes that hold up its arc: tyranny, the arrival of an equal, friendship, the great deed, loss, the flight from death and the return. The selection is editorial: the work preserves more episodes, variants and gaps than are shown here.

The spatial sequence was designed before the code. Each room answers to a state of the protagonist, and the progression from enclosed to open reproduces the movement of the poem: the hero loses power and gains understanding. The lapis lazuli thread in the floor is the only element that never changes along the way.

Original sources

The text

  • The Epic of Gilgamesh in its Standard Babylonian recension, transmitted on cuneiform tablets, together with the earlier Sumerian poems about Bilgames.
  • The quotation at each station is our own rendering into Spanish and English based on the public-domain English versions by W. Muss-Arnolt (1901) and M. Jastrow and A. T. Clay (1920). Those versions were chosen so as not to reproduce modern translations protected by copyright. The explanatory texts that accompany them are our own writing.

The archaeological artefacts

The journey includes photographs of six real artefacts: both faces of the Gilgamesh and Aga tablet, the seal of Mesannepada, Ninurta and Anzu, the scorpion-man and the Flood Tablet. They stand on lecterns between stations; a click opens each one with its name and provenance.

Cuneiform script

Tablet mode shows real cuneiform signs: the opening words of the poem, 𒊭𒅘𒁀𒄿𒈬𒊒 ša nag-ba i-mu-ru, “he who saw the Deep”; the Babylonian numerals of the stations; and proper names in their original spelling. Every sign has been checked against its name in the Unicode standard, and every spelling against published sources. It is a visual layer: not a translation of the texts into Akkadian.

The wall panels with columns of wedges are ornament generated by code and do not imitate legible writing.

The architecture

  • Documentation on Uruk and Mesopotamian building in mud brick and baked brick: buttressed and niched walls, stepped merlons, a terraced ziggurat with battered faces, cedar and palm beams.
  • The Ishtar Gate in Berlin’s Pergamonmuseum, as a reference for the blue glazed brick that announces the final transition.

Tools used

  • Image models: generation of the station reliefs, directed through descriptions written by the author. Each one carries its notice next to it.
  • Language models: support in writing, translation, structuring the script and writing code.
  • Three.js: construction of the three-dimensional space. All geometry is procedural, written in code; no third-party 3D models were used. The stone, mud-brick, brick, glazed-brick and wood textures are generated in the browser from height fields.
  • Web Audio: all sound is synthesised in the browser —the low drone, the wind, the fire, the rain and the thunder—; there are no recordings.
  • Typefaces: Cormorant Garamond, Alegreya Sans and Noto Sans Cuneiform, all under the SIL Open Font License and embedded in the page itself.

Privacy

The experience uses no cookies, loads no third-party resources —no fonts, libraries or analytics— and records no data about its visitors. The only thing it stores, in the browser itself, is whether Tablet mode is switched on.

Editorial criteria

  • Quotations are neither modernised nor abridged, and are always attributed to the versions they come from.
  • What is generated by code is never presented as a document: the cuneiform ornament on the walls is decorative, and the real signs of Tablet mode are identified with their transliteration.
  • The whole work can be read without starting the three-dimensional journey. The experience is a door, not a toll.
  • What is the author’s interpretation is presented as interpretation, not as archaeological fact.
  • The bull frieze on the glazed gate is drawn by code. Using photographs of the Pergamonmuseum reliefs was considered and rejected on licensing grounds: those available were under full copyright or under a licence that forbids derivative works. That the Babylonian relief is in the public domain through age is not enough: a photograph of a three-dimensional object creates its own rights for the photographer. The gate is not a replica of the Ishtar Gate but its grammar —blue, gold, courses and frieze— applied to the end of the journey.
  • Station XII is the subject of an explicit editorial decision, flagged here: Tablet XII is a late appendix, a translation of an independent Sumerian poem, and its dialogue with the spirit of Enkidu does not belong to the arc of the standard recension. The journey closes where the poem closes: with the return to Uruk and the walls as the only legacy.

Intellectual authorship and technical assistance

Intellectual authorship: the conception of the project, the selection of episodes, the design of the spatial sequence, the art direction, the writing and the review of all published content.

Technical assistance: the generation of drafts of text, images and code from the author’s instructions. Nothing is published without human review and editing.

In other words: the tools execute, they do not decide. The decisions about what is published, and the responsibility for it, are the author’s.

General notice

This experience was conceived, researched and directed by its author. Artificial intelligence tools were used as creative and technical support during design, programming, translation and production. The selection, organisation, review and editorial direction of the content are the author’s.
